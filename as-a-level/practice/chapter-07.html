Section A: Paper 1 style

Choose one answer for each question. Then press Mark my answers.

  1. In a transverse section of a young dicot root, where is the xylem found?

    Answer: C. Root vascular tissue is central; xylem forms a star with phloem between the arms.

  2. In a dicot stem, where are the vascular bundles?

    Answer: C. Bundles form a ring, with xylem towards the inside and phloem towards the outside.

  3. Which feature of xylem vessel elements helps them transport water?

    Answer: D. Dead, empty, continuous tubes with no end walls.

  4. What is the function of lignin in xylem vessel walls?

    Answer: A. Lignin is strong and waterproof.

  5. Which describes a sieve tube element?

    Answer: C. Sieve tube elements depend on companion cells.

  6. Why do companion cells have many mitochondria?

    Answer: B. ATP drives proton pumps for loading.

  7. What is the apoplast pathway?

    Answer: D. Water moves through the cellulose walls.

  8. What is the role of the Casparian strip in the endodermis?

    Answer: C. Suberin blocks the walls, so water must pass through cell membranes.

  9. What force mainly pulls water up the xylem?

    Answer: B. Evaporation from leaves creates tension; cohesion holds the column together.

  10. What causes cohesion between water molecules?

    Answer: D. Polar water molecules form hydrogen bonds with each other.

  11. Which condition increases the rate of transpiration?

    Answer: A. Wind removes water vapour, increasing the gradient.

  12. Why does transpiration rate increase in bright light?

    Answer: A. Stomata open for photosynthesis, letting water vapour out.

  13. What does a potometer measure directly?

    Answer: C. Water uptake ≈ transpiration, as most water taken up is lost.

  14. Which is a xerophytic adaptation of a leaf?

    Answer: A. Traps humid air, reducing the water potential gradient.

  15. Why do hairs on a xerophyte leaf reduce water loss?

    Answer: A. Reduces the diffusion gradient.

  16. What is translocation?

    Answer: C. Assimilates move from sources to sinks.

  17. Which is a sink?

    Answer: D. Sinks use or store assimilates.

  18. How is sucrose loaded into companion cells?

    Answer: A. Proton pump creates a gradient; H⁺ re-enter with sucrose.

  19. Why does water enter the sieve tube at the source?

    Answer: D. Water follows by osmosis, raising hydrostatic pressure.

  20. Phloem sap moves by mass flow because…

    Answer: B. High pressure at source, lower at sink.

Section B: Paper 2 style

Write your answers in your exercise book, then check against the mark scheme.

Question 1 [8 marks]

  1. Describe how the structure of xylem vessel elements is related to their function. [4]
  2. Describe the structure of a sieve tube element and its companion cell. [4]
Show mark scheme
  • (a) dead / no cytoplasm: no obstruction to flow; no end walls: continuous column; lignified walls: strength, resist collapse under tension, waterproof; pits: lateral movement; narrow: helps capillarity / cohesion [max 4]
  • (b) sieve tube: living, little cytoplasm, no nucleus, few organelles; sieve plates with pores at ends; companion cell: nucleus, dense cytoplasm, many mitochondria, ribosomes; linked by plasmodesmata [max 4]

Question 2 [8 marks]

  1. Describe the pathways by which water moves from root hair cells to the xylem. [4]
  2. Explain how water moves up the xylem from roots to leaves. [4]
Show mark scheme
  • (a) apoplast: through cell walls / spaces; symplast: through cytoplasm via plasmodesmata; (vacuolar route); down water potential gradient; Casparian strip in endodermis blocks apoplast; water must cross membranes into symplast [max 4]
  • (b) water evaporates from mesophyll cell walls and diffuses out through stomata (transpiration); water potential in leaf lowered; water moves from xylem into leaf cells; creates tension in xylem; cohesion: hydrogen bonds between water molecules; adhesion to xylem walls; continuous column pulled up (cohesion–tension); mass flow [max 4]

Question 3 [9 marks]

A student used a potometer to measure water uptake by a leafy shoot.

  1. State three precautions needed when setting up the potometer. [3]
  2. The air bubble moved 45 mm in 5 minutes. The capillary tube has a radius of 0.5 mm. Calculate the volume of water taken up per minute. (volume = πr²l) [2]
  3. Explain how two named features of xerophytic leaves reduce water loss. [4]
Show mark scheme
  • (a) cut shoot under water; at a slant; assemble under water / no air bubbles; seal joints with petroleum jelly; dry the leaves; allow to equilibrate before readings [max 3]
  • (b) π × 0.5² × 45 = 35.3 mm³ in 5 min; = 7.1 mm³ min⁻¹ [2]
  • (c) e.g. sunken stomata / rolled leaves / hairs: trap humid air, reduce water potential gradient, less diffusion; thick waxy cuticle: reduces evaporation from surface; few / small stomata: less area for diffusion; small leaves / needles: smaller surface area [two features explained, max 4]

Question 4 [8 marks]

  1. Explain what is meant by the terms source and sink. [2]
  2. Describe how sucrose is loaded into phloem and explain how it is moved to a sink. [6]
Show mark scheme
  • (a) source: where assimilates are made or released, e.g. photosynthesising leaf / storage organ; sink: where assimilates are used or stored, e.g. root, fruit, growing tip [2]
  • (b) H⁺ actively pumped out of companion cells using ATP; H⁺ gradient; H⁺ diffuse back in with sucrose via co-transporter protein; sucrose moves to sieve tube via plasmodesmata; lowers water potential; water enters by osmosis (from xylem); high hydrostatic pressure at source; sucrose removed at sink, water leaves, lower pressure; mass flow down pressure gradient [max 6]
